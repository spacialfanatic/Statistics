01 / PROBABILITY & STATISTICS

1. Theoretical background

Two random variables X and F are independent if knowing the value of one does not change the probability distribution of the other. In general, independence means P(X ∈ A, F ∈ B) = P(X ∈ A)P(F ∈ B) for every pair of measurable sets A and B. For discrete categorical variables, this reduces to the following equality for every possible pair of values:

P(X = x, F = f) = P(X = x) P(F = f)

Equivalently, P(F = f | X = x) = P(F = f), whenever P(X = x) > 0. In a finite sample, we estimate these probabilities using observed frequencies. Exact equality is not generally expected even when the population variables are independent, because of sampling variation.

5. Expected frequencies under independence

Under the null hypothesis, the expected joint frequency is the sample size multiplied by the product of the marginal proportions.

06 / THE HYPOTHESIS TEST

6. Pearson's chi-square test

H₀: X and F are independent. H₁: X and F are not independent.

χ² = Σi Σj (Oij − Eij)² / Eij
df = (r − 1)(c − 1)

A large statistic indicates a greater discrepancy between the observed and expected counts. The p-value is the probability, under H₀ and the chi-square approximation, of a statistic at least as large as the observed one. We use a significance level of α = 0.05.

7. Interpretation and conclusion

Statistical association does not imply causality. In the example, differences in phishing responses could be related to prior experience, job roles, selection bias or other factors. Even if the click rate decreases across training categories, this table alone cannot show that training caused the decrease. A causal claim would require an appropriate research design, such as random assignment, and additional assumptions.

Independence is a property of a probability distribution. A hypothesis test can provide evidence against independence, but failure to reject H₀ does not prove that independence holds.
